What are the Wonders of the World?

The exact history of the term "Seven Wonders of the World" is somewhat confusing. The earliest known reference to these seven ancient wonders (as we know them today) was in a list written by the epigrammist Antipater of Sidon, likely before 100 BC. It goes:

"I have gazed on the walls of impregnable Babylon along which chariots may race, and on the Zeus by the banks of the Alpheus, I have seen the hanging gardens, and the Colossus of the Helios, the great man-made mountains of the lofty pyramids, and the gigantic tomb of Mausolus; but when I saw the sacred house of Artemis that towers to the clouds, the others were placed in the shade, for the sun himself has never looked upon its equal outside Olympus."

As we see, this list contains 6/7 of what we now consider the seven ancient wonders, but substituted the Lighthouse of Alexandria for the walls of Babylon. Over the coming centuries, several similar lists would appear, all with different "wonders" or "sights" listed. Some other notable landmarks that were at one point considered included Cyrus' Palace, the Cyzicus Temple, and the Heraclea Temple. The exact list that we have today was developed in a 1572 publication Octo Mundi Miracula, which drew from a variety of historical sources.

The seven ancient wonders were all magnificent works that displayed humanity's creativity and abilities. However, there were two main issues. For one, the list was centered on a very specific part of the world. We can see that all seven were either in Greece, Turkey, Iraq, or Egypt. This meant that other notable structures from Asia, southern Africa, or the Americas were omitted. Additionally, by the time the official list was crafted in the 16th century, most of the wonders no longer stood. Historians don't even have a very solid understanding of what the Hanging Gardens looked like, for example.

With these issues in mind, a vote was held in 2007 to determine the seven "modern" wonders of the world. The poll was held by a private Swiss foundation, and it garnered over 100 million votes. The new list was much more representative of the entire world, and every structure on the list is also still standing, which makes it much more interesting for tourists. However, the new list was still criticized for being unscientific and biased. Critics argue that the list was too heavily influenced by internet votes, omitting structures such as Angkor Wat in Cambodia, a country which, at the time, did not have widely available internet like many of these other countries.

At the end of the day, it is important to remember that these seven ancient wonders, and the seven modern wonders, are simply a fun way to recognize some of humanity's great significance. The lists are in no way trying to determine the seven greatest feats of engineering, or the seven most culturally significant places in human history, or anything like that. With that being said, it is still cool to have a list of locations like this that we can map!
